The form

am / is / are + -ing

Take the verb you already know — am, is or are — and add a second verb with -ing on the end.

I
↓
am
+ working
he, she, it
↓
is
+ eating
we, you, they
↓
are
+ running
!

The -ing verb never changes. Only am / is / are changes.

Meaning
The most important idea

It means now

Something is happening at this moment — while you are speaking.

  • Please be quiet. I’m working. (= I’m working now)
  • Look, there’s Sarah. She’s wearing a brown coat. (= she is wearing it now)
  • The weather is nice. It’s not raining.
  • “Where are the children?” “They’re playing in the park.”
✓

If you can point at it and it is still moving — use am / is / are + -ing.

In real life

Where you will hear it

This is the tense of the phone call, the window and the photograph — any time the other person cannot see what you see.

On the phone

  • We’re having dinner now. Can I call you later?
  • You can turn off the television. I’m not watching it.

Looking out of a window

  • It’s raining. The sun isn’t shining.
  • Look! Somebody is swimming in the river.
Fluency · Language Mastery07
Fluency • Lesson 3
Negatives

Saying what is not happening

Put not after am, is or are — exactly as you did in Lesson 1. Nothing else moves.

  • I’m not watching TV.
  • She isn’t eating. (or She’s not eating.)
  • You aren’t listening to me. (or You’re not listening.)
  • They aren’t walking. They’re running.
!

With I there is only one form: I’m not. There is no “I amn’t”.

Fluency · Language Mastery08
Fluency • Lesson 3
Spelling

Spelling the -ing form

Most verbs simply take -ing: play → playing, eat → eating, watch → watching. There are three small changes to learn.

1 · Verbs ending in -e lose the e
come→coming
write→writing
dance→dancing
2 · Short verbs double the last letter
run→running
sit→sitting
swim→swimming
3 · Verbs ending in -ie become -ying
lie→lying
die→dying
!

Do not drop the e in verbs like see → seeing or agree → agreeing. Only a silent e disappears.

Talking About Now

There is a moment every learner remembers: the first time they described something in English without translating it first. Usually it happens by accident. You look out of a window, you see rain, and the words arrive on their own — it’s raining.

That is what the present continuous gives you. It is the tense of the window, the photograph and the phone call. You use it when the thing you are describing is in front of you and still moving. She’s waiting for a bus. They’re playing football. The bus is coming.

Beginners often avoid it because it looks like two verbs at once. It is not. It is the verb you already know — am, is, are — with a second word attached. Nothing new to memorise, only one small habit to build.

So practise it where it belongs: out loud, in real time. Sit somewhere with people, and quietly describe what they are doing. Nobody has to hear you. Within a week the sentences stop being grammar and start being speech.
