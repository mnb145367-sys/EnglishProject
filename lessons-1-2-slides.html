The verb

One verb, three forms

English changes this verb depending on who you are talking about. There are only three forms to learn.

I
↓
am
he, she, it
↓
is
we, you, they
↓
are
!

Notice that you always takes are — whether you mean one person or many.

Short forms

Short forms

In conversation, English speakers almost always join the two words together.

I am→I’m
we are→we’re
he is→he’s
you are→you’re
she is→she’s
they are→they’re
it is→it’s
Writing the long form is not wrong — it just sounds formal.

Negatives

Saying no: add not

To make a negative, put not straight after am, is or are. Nothing else changes.

  • I am not tired.
  • He is not at school.
  • They are not ready.
Fluency · Language Mastery08
Fluency • Lesson 1
Negatives

Two ways to shorten a negative

Both are correct. Choose whichever feels easier to say.

he is not→he’s not
he is not→he isn’t
we are not→we’re not
we are not→we aren’t
!

I is the exception: only I’m not is possible. There is no form “I amn’t”.

that’s · there’s · here’s

These three short forms appear constantly in daily conversation.

  • Look! There’s your bus.
  • Here’s your coffee.

The rule

The one rule: swap two words

Put am, is or are before the person.

You are ready.→Are you ready?
She is a teacher.→Is she a teacher?
I am late.→Am I late?
They are at home.→Are they at home?
!

That is the whole rule. Nothing else in the sentence moves.

Fluency · Language Mastery21
Fluency • Lesson 2
Question words

Which word do you need?

Choose the question word by what you want to know.

What→a thing
How→a condition (fine, tired, cold)
Who→a person
How old→an age
Where→a place
Why → a reason: Why are you late?
Fluency · Language Mastery22
Fluency • Lesson 2
Short forms

Where’s · What’s · Who’s

In speaking, the question word joins with is.

Where is→Where’s
What is→What’s
Who is→Who’s
How is→How’s
  • Where’s the bus stop?
  • What’s your job?
  • Who’s that woman?
!

There is no short form for are. We say “Where are you from?” — never “Where’re”.

Fluency · Language Mastery23
Fluency • Lesson 2
Answering

Short answers

Do not answer only Yes or No — it sounds rude. Add the person and the verb.

Are you married?→Yes, I am. / No, I’m not.
Is it cold today?→Yes, it is. / No, it isn’t.
Are your hands cold?→Yes, they are. / No, they aren’t.
!

Never shorten a positive short answer. Say “Yes, I am.” — not “Yes, I’m.” Negative answers can be short: “No, he isn’t.”

Asking Good Questions

Many learners spend months studying English but stay silent in conversation. They know hundreds of words, yet when they meet someone new, they say nothing. The problem is usually not vocabulary. It is that they never practised asking.

A question does two useful things at once. It keeps the conversation alive, and it gives you a free minute to listen instead of speak. Good speakers are not the ones who talk the most — they are the ones who ask well and then pay attention to the answer.

The good news for a beginner is that the most common English questions are also the simplest. Where are you from? What’s your name? How old is your brother? Is this seat free? Every one of them is built from am, is or are, plus a small change in word order. Learn that pattern and you already have the key to a real conversation.

So the next time you meet someone, do not wait until your English is perfect. Ask one question. Then listen, and ask another.
